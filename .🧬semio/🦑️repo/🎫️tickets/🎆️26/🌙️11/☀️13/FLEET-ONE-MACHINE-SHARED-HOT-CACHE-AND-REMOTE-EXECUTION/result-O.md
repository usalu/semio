## (3) Economics, from the 441-unit baseline (p50 757 ms, sum 688 s, p90 3.6 s, max 32.5 s)
| unit cost threshold | units | share of compile time | local seconds freed (cost minus 2.5 s) |
|---|---|---|---|
| >= 1.5 s | 105 | 71.0 % | 252 |
| >= 2.5 s | 63 | 59.5 % (409 of 688 s) | 252 |
| >= 4 s | 38 | 48.2 % | 237 |
| >= 8 s | 14 | 29.5 % | 168 |
- 378 of 441 units (86 %) cost less than the 2.5 s round trip; 63 units hold 60 % of the compile time. Offloading pays for units above about 2.5 s (above 4 s the gain per unit is more than 40 %) and only when the machine is the bottleneck: a machine with free cores and memory compiles a 3 s unit in 3 s locally; the offload costs 1.7 to 3 s of latency but no local CPU. It pays when (a) the local queue or memory wait is longer than the round trip (the baseline ran with 1 to 2 admitted jobs: 640 s wall for 688 s of work, i.e. the machine was the bottleneck), (b) the minis have headroom (3 of 6 did), (c) the unit is large. It never pays for the 86 % small units, and a CPU-saturated mini makes the round trip longer (up to 17 s for queued build scripts).
- What the fleet gains from each: the shared cache turns a unit into a 10 to 100 ms fetch for every machine after the first (the second crate: 519 of 643 units were fleet hits in 90 s; a cold 441-unit build took 640 s), so for repeated work it is worth a hundred times more than offload. Offload only helps the FIRST compile of a unit (cold fleet), moves at most the ~60 % in large units, costs 1.7 s+ each, and doubles the places that must be byte-identical. Expected best case for a cold build like this one: about 250 s of local CPU freed of 688 s (36 %), less whatever the minis' own load costs; measured end to end: not demonstrated (the accepted units never exceeded 14 per run).
