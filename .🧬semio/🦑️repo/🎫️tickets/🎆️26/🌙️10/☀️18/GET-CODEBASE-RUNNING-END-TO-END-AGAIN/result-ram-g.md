## Simulation (C/ram-g-tools/simulate.py, output `C/🗑️generated/ram-g/sim.txt`, 5 seeds, mean)
Model: trace requests of 10-24 16:00 - 10-25 08:00 (165 queued requests, real total wait 15.7 h, p50 124 s, p90 670 s); actual peak/duration of each request drawn from the measured grants of its operation (4 peak strata = 4 classes per operation); a grant idles 10-60 % of its time (lent after the policy delay) then ramps to its peak in 30 s; foreign memory is an AR(1) process (not ours: editors, agents' clients, browsers); the decision is the Python port; learning starts EMPTY. Reading: queue time falls 2.4x (replay) and 2.3x (16 GB, same agents) while the minutes under 6 % free memory stay equal (replay) or rise by ~10 % (16 GB: more heavy graph builds are really running; the foreign process is exogenous in the model). The ablation shows where the gain comes from: floor and shorter ramp/lend give 2.4x alone on 32 GB; measured demand adds the honesty (heavy classes reserve up to 2x their default instead of 1x, the cause of the swap storms) and the 16 GB hot rebuild. Allowing learned ratios up to 4x (first version) made the heaviest stratum starve (mean 560 s vs 49 s) and was cut to 2x / 50 % share; capping at 1.0x gave the lowest waits but +37 % minutes under 6 % free (unprotected overcommit). A developer's hot rebuild waits 3 s median, p90 -31 % with the interactive class.
Limits: the model replaces the unknown real arrivals of agents by the first arrival of each log; waits are open-loop; the foreign process is stationary; critical minutes are dominated by the foreign process. Numbers are comparisons between policies on one trace, not forecasts.
